Abstract
Background Airway basal cells have been shown to play important roles in lung disease, prompting their use in 
research involving 3-dimensional (3D) organoid culture. Mouse tracheal basal cells (MTBC) represent a key model 
in understanding the differentiation of airway basal cells in normal development and diseased states. The isolation, 
expansion, and culture media of basal cells can affect their capacity to maintain stemness and properly differentiate, 
thereby introducing unknown experimental variables. As MTBC use in 3D organoid culture proceeds, there is an 
increasing need for comparative studies to identify unknown experimental variables. In this study, we examined the 
effects of MTBC expansion media on the differentiation and tracheosphere-forming ability in 3D culture.
Methods MTBC were isolated from mouse tracheas, and three commonly used airway basal cell media were 
compared during MTBC expansion in cell culture (hereafter referred to as Media 1, 2, and 3). Following expansion, 
MTBC were cultured identically in 3D conditions to analyze tracheosphere-forming capability. Image analyses were 
performed to characterize tracheosphere count, size, and morphology, while metabolic and transcriptomic signatures 
were analyzed to assess MTBC differentiation in 3D culture.
Results Expansion of MTBC in the three distinct media resulted in subsequent 3D tracheospheres that displayed 
unique metabolite and gene expression profiles, accompanied by overall changes in tracheosphere count and size. 
MTBC expanded in Medium 1 displayed lower levels of TCA cycle intermediates, along with lowered expression of 
differentiation markers Foxj1 and Scgb1a1, compared to MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3. Pathway analysis showed an 
upregulation of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis signaling pathways in tracheospheres cultured from MTBC expanded in 
Media 2 or 3.
Conclusion This study identified several key differences in tracheosphere-forming ability of MTBC resulting from 
MTBC expansion media and highlights the importance of transparency in cell culture protocols, particularly those 
involving stem cells or 3D culture.

Introduction
Organoid culture is an increasingly popular method for 
biomedical research due to its in vivo biological relevance 
and expanding potential for personalized therapy. Unlike 
conventional methods of cell culture involving cells 
grown in two-dimensions on stiff plastic dishes, organoid 
culture better mimics the pliable environment of human 
tissue and allows stem or stem-like cells to undergo 
morphogenesis, whereby the cells self-organize, differ -
entiate, and form 3-dimensional (3D) structures with 
greater resemblance to their behavior in vivo [ 1]. Lung 
organoid cultures have been used to advance the study of 
lung injury and regeneration [ 2], disease [ 3], viral infec -
tion [4], and personalized medicine [ 5]. Lung organoids 
can be initiated from several types of cells with stem/
progenitor potential, including proximal and distal lung 
cells. Alveolar type 2 cells, the primary progenitor cell of 
alveoli, can be isolated and grown as organoid cultures 
to model lower airway structures [ 6], while airway basal 
cells can be isolated and cultured to give rise to tracheo -
spheres and/or bronchospheres, consisting of basal cells 
surrounding a differentiated, pseudostratified epithe -
lium of ciliated and secretory cells [ 7]. A diversity exists 
in protocols used for isolation, expansion, and growth of 
airway progenitor cells in 3D culture, including protocols 
for the isolation of airway basal cells from both human 
and mouse lungs [ 7– 9]. However, the isolation methods, 
basal cell markers used for sorting, conditions of basal 
cell expansion, and specifics of seeding into Matrigel 
(cell number, volume, air-liquid interface or submerged) 
all differ among these protocols [ 10– 13], making cross-
study comparisons difficult.
There is also a need for the characterization of diverse 
endpoint analyses resulting from 3D airway cultures [ 14, 
15]. A commonly reported biological outcome is the 
characterization of cell types using immunofluorescence 
staining or gene expression analysis, including costly 
single-cell RNA sequencing. Lower cost endpoints that 
can still resolve biological differences and allow for con -
tinuous monitoring of 3D cultures without harvesting are 
desirable. Additionally, colony forming efficiency (CFE), 
calculated from the number of spheres that grow com -
pared to total cells seeded, can be used as a measure of 
progenitor potential [1]. Manual quantification of CFE is 
time-consuming and vulnerable to between-individual 
variability in annotations. Now, machine learning and 
computer algorithms have the potential to reduce image 
analysis efforts, while providing analysis standardization 
and more detailed morphological information from each 
image.
In the present descriptive study, we sought to directly 
compare three different protocols for the expansion of 
mouse tracheal basal cells (MTBC), with the goal of 
retaining potential for growth and differentiation in 3D 
culture by varying the medium used during the expan -
sion step. We tested three different media from cited lit -
erature for the growth of MTBC [ 9, 16, 17]. Further, we 
describe the use of an in silico method to quantify tra -
cheosphere CFE and characterize colonies by size and 
morphology. Finally, we utilized metabolomics and bulk 
RNA sequencing to further comparatively assess tracheo-
sphere cultures. Results from these studies demonstrate 
that the choice of expansion medium plays a crucial role 
in the cellular composition and metabolic characteristics 
of tracheospheres.
Methods
Cell isolation and expansion
All animal procedures were carried out at the University 
of Vermont (UVM) Animal Care Facility, and approved 
by the UVM Institutional Animal Care and Use Com -
mittee (IACUC #202000035). Mouse tracheal basal 
cells (MTBC) were isolated as previously described 
[18]. Briefly, 8 to 12 week old C57BL/6NJ mice ( n = 9 
total) were euthanized through intraperitoneal injec -
tion of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) and the tracheas were 
exposed. Tracheas were tied off at one end and filled with 
a protease solution containing 0.1% protease type 14 
(Sigma p-5147), 2 µL/mL primocin, and 10 µL/mL pen/
strep in Opti-MEM (Gibco 31985–062). Tracheas were 
tied at the opposite end and incubated in protease solu -
tion overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, tracheas 
were cut on one end and flushed with DMEM containing 
10% fetal bovine serum into a 50 mL conical tube. Mouse 
tracheas were flushed and pooled, divided into 3 tubes, 
centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, and each tube resuspended 
in one of three experimental media (Fig. 1A and B). Each 
medium contained DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330–032), 
50 U/mL Penicillin, and 50 µg/mL streptomycin with the 
following individual additions:
1. Medium 1: 4.5 mM L-glutamine, 100 µg/mL 
Primocin, 20 ng/mL Cholera Toxin, 4 µg/mL Insulin, 
5 µg/mL Transferrin, 25 µg/mL Bovine Pituitary 
Extract, 10 ng/mL Epidermal Growth Factor, 100 nM 
Dexamethasone.
2. Medium 2: 4 mM L-glutamine, 0.25 µg/mL 
Fungizone, 0.1 µg/mL Cholera Toxin, 10 µg/mL 
Insulin, 5 µg/mL Transferrin, 30 µg/mL Bovine 
Pituitary Extract, 25 ng/mL Epidermal Growth 
Factor, 0.01 µM Retinoic Acid, 5% Fetal Bovine 
Serum.
3. Medium 3: All components in Medium 2 plus 1 µM 
DMH-1 (ALK2 inhibitor), 1 µM A-83-01 (TGF-βRI, 
ALK4, and ALK7 inhibitor), 10 µM Y27632 (ROCK-I 
and ROCK-II inhibitor).

Cells were seeded into T-75 flasks precoated with col -
lagen gels (18–20 mg/mL, Corning 354236). Each con -
dition was cultured in their respective medium until 
confluency, approximately 10 days.
Cell passaging and 3D culture
MTBC were passaged using consecutive collagenase 
(0.4 mg/mL, 2 hrs) and trypsin (2.5%, 5 min) digestions. 
Cells from each expansion condition were separately 
brought to a single cell suspension in Pneumacult ALI 
Medium (Stemcell Technologies, 05001) at 250,000 cells/
mL. Pneumacult ALI Medium was utilized for 3D cul -
ture growth as it has been previously determined to be 
optimal for supporting differentiation of airway basal 
cells [ 19]. For seeding in 3D culture, 12 mm transwell 
inserts (Corning, 3401) were first coated with 100 µL of 
a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Corning, 356231) and Pneu -
macult ALI Medium, then left to solidify at 37 °C, 5% 
CO2 for 20 min, thereby creating a barrier preventing cell 
attachment to the transwell membrane. 50,000 MTBC in 
200 µL Pneumacult ALI Medium were mixed on ice with 
an equal volume of Matrigel to a final volume of 400 µL, 
which was then immediately seeded on top of the solidi -
fied Matrigel coating in each well and incubated at 37 °C, 
5% CO2. Importantly, the time between seeding the cells 
in Matrigel and placing in 37 °C, 5% CO 2 was minimized 
to less than 30 seconds to prevent cells from sinking to 
the lower parts of the transwell before Matrigel solidifi -
cation. Cells suspended in Matrigel were then left at 37 
°C, 5% CO 2 for 45 minutes to allow Matrigel solidifica -
tion, after which Pneumacult ALI Medium was added 
to the basolateral chamber and changed every other day 
throughout the duration of the experiment. Tracheo -
spheres were imaged throughout their growth using an 
I×70Light Microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell harvest
Tracheosphere cultures were harvested through diges -
tion of the Matrigel using Cell Recovery Solution (Corn -
ing, 354253). Matrigel was resuspended in cell recovery 
solution and incubated on ice for 20 min. Tracheospheres 
were moved to a 1.5 mL tube and centrifuged at 300 ×g 
for 2 minutes. The supernatant was aspirated from the 
pelleted tracheospheres, which were washed with ice cold 
PBS and centrifuged in the same manner. The final pellet 
was resuspended in RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen, 79216) to 
isolate RNA.
RNA extraction and qPCR
Total RNA was isolated using QIAshredder (Qia -
gen,79654) and RNeasy Kits (Qiagen, 74104) according to 
manufacturer’s directions. cDNA was synthesized from 
1 µg RNA. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) of individual genes 
related to proliferation and differentiation ( Krt5, Foxj1, 
Scgb1a1, and Mki67 ) was performed using TaqMan 
gene analysis using SYBR Green (BioRad, Hercules, CA). 
Primers used for each gene are reported in Supplemental 
Table 1.
Organoid segmentation and quantitative morphological 
analysis
Image acquisition of tracheosphere cultures was per -
formed on days 4, 7, 12, and 19, with three replicate 
images taken per medium condition (n = 36 images total). 
Organoid counting and size estimation on each image 
was performed using OrgaQuant, an organoid localiza -
tion and quantification algorithm using deep convo -
lutional neural networks [ 14]. OrgaQuant was used to 
superimpose boundary boxes on tracheospheres, with 
an empirically settled confidence threshold of 0.85, and 
outliers (i.e. unreasonably large boundary boxes that did 
not capture an individual tracheosphere) were manually 
removed from the analyses.
A custom algorithm scripted in MatLab (The Math -
works, Natick, MA) was used to quantitatively analyze 
the tracheosphere images after OrgaQuant processing. 
Individual tracheosphere area was calculated based on 
the average length of the OrgaQuant bounding boxes 
(corresponding to tracheosphere diameter). The total 
tracheosphere area was calculated as the sum of the area 
of individual tracheospheres divided by the size of the 
image. Tracheosphere areas in all 27 images were then 
normalized by the area of the largest organoid within the 
dataset (89462 pixels 2), and an empirical cumulative dis -
tribution function (CDF) was generated. Lastly, the area 
under each CDF curve (AUC) was calculated. The total 
tracheosphere area was normalized on the x-axis by the 
size of the largest tracheosphere in the dataset (89462 
pixels2) for all cases. Shapiro-Wilk normality tests were 
performed for each set of repetitions on each day and 
media with p > 0.05 for all cases. 1-way ANOVA was sub -
sequently performed to compare intra-day differences 
between conditions on culture days 7, 12, and 19. Upon 
finding a statistically significant ANOVA result (p < 0.05), 
a post-hoc Tukey test was performed to identify which 
specific group means were significantly different from 
each other.
Metabolite analysis
Lactate levels were measured in conditioned media after 
14 days in culture using the L-Lactate Assay Kit (Eton 
Bioscience). Metabolomics analysis was performed on 
conditioned media also after 14 days in culture by liq -
uid chromatography/mass spectrometry at the Uni -
versity of Colorado School of Medicine Metabolomics 
Facility at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medi -
cal Campus (Aurora, CO). Metabolites were extracted 
from conditioned media samples using cold 5:3:2 
methanol:acetonitrile:water at a ratio of 1 part condi -
tioned media to 24 parts extraction solution exactly as 
previously described [ 20]. Extracts were analyzed on a 
Thermo Vanquish UHPLC coupled to a Thermo Q Exac-
tive mass spectrometer using a 5 minute C18 gradient in 
positive and negative ion modes (separate runs). Quality 
control, metabolite annotation, and peak area integration 
were performed using El-Maven (Elucidata) as described 
[20]. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, 
with the majority of metabolites exhibiting normal dis -
tributions. Significantly changed metabolites ( p ≤ 0.05) 
were determined using one way ANOVA with post-hoc 
pairwise t-tests and correction for multiple comparisons 
(full statistics results in Supplementary Data File 1). Heat 
maps including significantly changed metabolites were 
created by using a range scale function to scale the data 
set and visualized using the pheatmap package (version 
1.0.13) in R (version 4.5.0) [ 21]. Significantly changed 
metabolites were used as input in Ingenuity Pathway 
Analysis (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) to identify canoni -
cal pathways that were significantly changed between 
expansion medium conditions.
RNA sequencing, data processing, and analysis
RNA extracted from tracheosphere cultures after 14 days 
in culture (described above) was sent to Novogene (Sac -
ramento, CA) for library prep, quality control, and bulk 
RNA sequencing analysis. FASTQ files were aligned 
using STAR (v2.7.10a) as previously described [ 22]. Sam-
ples were run across two lanes and resulting counts were 
added together after alignment. Raw count data were 
filtered to remove genes that had zero reads across all 
samples and lanes, resulting in matrix containing 15,028 
genes. Counts from the two lanes were added within each 
sample to generate the final count matrix used as input 
for differential gene expression analysis.

Differential gene expression was assessed using the 
DESeq2 package (version 1.48.1) [ 23] and published 
workflow [24]. In preliminary analyses, we also explored 
removing unwanted experimental variation using the 
RUVg approach within the RUVSeq package (version 
1.42.0) [25]; however, this approach resulted in minimal 
changes to the list of significantly differentially expressed 
genes. Therefore, we proceeded with DESeq2 using 
medium condition as the only element in the design 
equation. Genes with Benjamini Hochberg adjusted 
p-value < 0.05 and DESeq2-calculated log2 fold change 
(FC) > +/- 1.5 (fold change > +/- 2.25) were considered 
significantly differentially expressed. Principal compo -
nent analysis was performed using the factoextra pack -
age (version 1.0.7) [ 26] using DESeq2 normalized counts 
as input. To identify biological pathways associated with 
these differentially expressed genes, pathway enrichment 
analysis was carried out using Ingenuity Pathway Analy -
sis. Pathway enrichment statistics were calculated based 
off a modified Fischer’s Exact test, adjusted for multiple 
testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction as 
previously described [ 27] and as described by Ingenu -
ity Pathway Analysis documentation. Z-scores were also 
produced per enriched pathway reflecting predicted acti -
vation (positive z-score) or inhibition (negative z-score) 
for each pathway. Results of these analyses were visual -
ized using pheatmap [21] and ggplot2 (version 3.5.2) [28].

Results
Expansion medium influences tracheosphere-forming 
potential of isolated MTBC
Herein, we set out to perform a side-by-side comparison 
of three different MTBC expansion media from previ -
ously published protocols to determine how expansion 
media composition impacts subsequent growth and dif -
ferentiation in 3D culture. MTBC were isolated from 
C57BL/6NJ mouse tracheas, pooled, and split into three 
experimental groups to be expanded in collagen gel-
coated T75 flasks using one of three different media 
(Fig. 1A and B). Following expansion, we next assessed 
how MTBC were able to differentiate when grown in 3D 
culture in Matrigel at air-liquid interface (ALI). MTBC 
were seeded in 50% Matrigel/Pneumacult ALI Medium 
in transwells and cultured at ALI with Pneumacult ALI 
Medium in the basolateral chamber (Fig. 1A). Cultures 
were imaged every few days to monitor the growth and 
morphogenesis of tracheospheres. As shown in Fig. 2, 
cells expanded in Media 2 or 3 formed a greater num -
ber of larger tracheospheres at an earlier time point than 
those expanded in Medium 1. This difference was partic -
ularly evident at day 12, when tracheospheres expanded 
in Media 2 or 3 show a greater number of and larger tra -
cheospheres than those expanded in Medium 1 (Figs. 2, 
3).
Next, we characterized tracheosphere growth using 
OrgaQuant to count and categorize spheres by size (Fig. 
3A). A custom algorithm was developed to calculate the 
total tracheosphere area and size distribution (MatLab 
2022, The Mathworks, Natick, MA). The total tracheo -
sphere area for each expansion Medium condition was 
then normalized to the total image area, and showed 
that expansion Media 2 and 3 resulted in a significantly 
greater area of tracheospheres per image at Day 12, com -
pared to expansion Medium 1 (Fig. 3B). At Day 19, all 
expansion media conditions showed an increased area 
of tracheospheres per image compared to Day 7, with 
Medium 3 showing a significantly greater area of tra -
cheospheres per image than Media 1 or 2. To differenti -
ate between cultures containing a large number of small 
tracheospheres and a small number of large tracheo -
spheres, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of 
tracheosphere size and 1–area under the curve (AUC) 
were calculated. Results presented in Fig. 3C and D show 
variations in the y-axis related to the size distribution of 
the tracheospheres. In this manner, left-skewed distri -
butions were mainly composed of small tracheospheres 
while right-skewed distributions were dominated by 
larger tracheospheres. The CDF calculations revealed 
that expansion in Media 2 or 3 resulted in cultures 
mainly composed of tracheospheres with larger areas at 
Day 12, compared to expansion Medium 1 (Fig. 3C), as 
quantified by 1-AUC (Fig. 3D). At Day 19, images of all 
expansion media conditions showed increased content 
of tracheospheres with larger areas compared to Day 7, 
with Medium 3 showing significantly higher composi -
tion of larger tracheospheres than Media 1 or 2. Total 
area and 1-AUC present different, albeit complementary, 
information. While total area captures the total tracheo -
sphere content in the image, it does not discern whether 
there is a larger number of small tracheospheres or a 
smaller number of large tracheospheres. On the other 
hand, 1-AUC does not provide the total tracheosphere 
content in the well but can detect whether the image is 
mainly composed of small tracheospheres (small 1-AUC) 
or large tracheospheres (large 1-AUC).

MTBC expanded in medium with FBS are more capable of 
subsequent differentiation in 3D culture
We next used quantitative PCR to measure expression of 
genes commonly implicated in proliferation and differen-
tiation of tracheosphere cultures after 14 and 21 days of 
growth. MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3 showed signifi -
cantly greater expression of Foxj1 and Scgb1a1 as com -
pared to MTBC expanded in Medium 1, suggesting that 
the former two conditions resulted in increased differ -
entiation into ciliated and secretory cells after 14 days in 
3D culture (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, tracheospheres from 
MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3 had significantly lower 
expression of Mki67, a marker associated with prolifera -
tion, at Day 14, and those from Medium 2 retained sig -
nificantly lower expression than those from Medium 1 at 
Day 21 (Fig. 4B). Taken together, these data indicate that 
at day 14, MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3 had prolif -
erated and then proceeded to differentiate, while MTBC 
expanded in Medium 1 remained proliferative. After 
21 days in culture, cells from all expansion conditions 
had similar expression levels of Foxj1, while expression 
of Scgb1a1 remained significantly elevated at Day 21 in 
cells expanded in Medium 2, as compared to Media 1 or 
3 (Fig. 4B).

diate lactate in medium from each condition after 14 and 
20 days in 3D culture using a commercially available lac -
tate assay. Tracheospheres from cells expanded in Media 
2 and 3 produced significantly greater lactate at both of 
these time points (Supplementary Fig. 1). With this evi -
dence that metabolite concentrations were significantly

RNA sequencing reveals changes related to pulmonary 
fibrosis signaling pathways
We next performed RNA sequencing on tracheospheres 
after 14 days in culture, a timepoint at which we observed 
significant changes in gene expression via qPCR, to fur -
ther characterize differences between expansion media. 
Principal component analysis of gene expression showed 
clustering of replicates from each expansion media, and 
further emphasized the similarity between expansion 
Media 2 and 3 when compared to Media 1 (Dimension 
1, 83.3%) (Fig. 6A). Differential gene expression between 
groups was then assessed using DESeq2 for pairwise 
comparisons of each expansion condition. Comparison 
of Medium 1 with 2 or 3 revealed the most differentially 
expressed genes (DEG) (7,437 and 8,643, respectively), 
with a majority of those being upregulated (Supplemen -
tary Fig. 2). There were comparatively fewer DEG (2,176) 
between Media 2 and 3, with the top 15 genes with the 
lowest p-value from each pairwise comparison shown 
in Fig. 6B. Gene expression of Krt5, Foxj1, Scgb1a1,  
and Mki67 among the three expansion conditions as 
measured by RNA sequencing closely matched values 
measured via qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 3).
We next identified significantly changed canonical sig -
naling pathways with underlying gene expression that 
differed between each MTBC expansion medium in 
resultant tracheospheres (Fig. 6 C–E). Surprisingly, when 
comparing expansion Medium 1 with Media 2 or 3, the 
most significantly changed pathways were involved in 
hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), 
with these pathways being upregulated in Media 2 and 
3. Aberrant basal cell differentiation and the presence of 
basaloid cells have recently been appreciated in IPF, and 
dedifferentiated basal cells are implicated in disease pro -
gression [ 33]. Additionally, pathways related to wound 
healing and fibroblast signaling were upregulated in 
Medium 2 compared to Medium 1 (Fig. 6 C and D), both 
of which are pathways implicated in fibrotic diseases. In 
comparison, pathways related to fibrotic signaling were 
not significantly different between expansion Media 2 
and 3 (Fig. 6E).

in the field of organoid research [12, 13, 38]. Additionally, 
single-cell sequence analysis of human nasal or bronchial 
epithelial cells grown in 2D ALI conditions has shown 
that media composition directly affects differentiation

with visible lumens at days 12 and 19, suggesting that

cell-cell junctions were intact. There was no significant 
difference in expression of the basal cell marker Krt5

previous reports showing that even after differentiation 
tracheospheres contain an exterior layer of basal cells [7].
Single-cell RNA sequencing is commonly used to 
characterize organoid cultures; however, it can be cost-
prohibitive. There is also a need for diverse readouts 
that allow for continuous monitoring of organoid cul -
tures and are less expensive alternatives to single-cell 
RNA sequencing. We employed targeted metabolomics 
on conditioned medium from cultures at Day 14, which 
revealed significant differences in metabolic profiles. Tra-
cheospheres from expansion Media 2 and 3 had higher 
levels of glycolysis and TCA cycle intermediates, while 
those expanded in Medium 1 had greater turnover of 
amino acids. Additionally, we performed RNA sequenc -
ing on cultures at the same timepoint, which even at 
a bulk level revealed significant differences between 
conditions. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis revealed that, 
along with decreases in the expression of differentia -
tion markers Foxj1 and Scgb1a1, tracheospheres grown 
from MTBC expanded in Medium 1 had significantly 
upregulated expression of certain genes associated with 
IPF when compared to tracheospheres grown from 
MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3, including Col1a1, 
Wnt10b, and Fzd10. Interestingly, tracheospheres grown 
from MTBC expanded in Media 2 or 3 had other signifi -
cantly upregulated genes that were associated with IPF, 
including Wnt5a, Wnt11, Col5a3, and Col6a1. Given the 
important roles that have been shown for airway basal 
cells during IPF pathogenesis, including their effects on 
lung fibroblasts [ 33], these data suggest the importance 
of consideration of expansion media when addressing 
these research questions.
Species differences and the characterization of subpop-
ulations among airway stem cells add to the methodolog-
ical complexity associated with airway organoid research. 
This is particularly true with airway basal cells, which are 
located in the mouse trachea but in humans throughout 
the trachea and upper bronchioles [ 41]. The heterogene-
ity among airway basal cells in healthy and diseased lungs 
has recently been realized, primarily through the use of 
single cell RNA sequencing, with markers such as NGFR, 
Keratin 5 (KRT5), Keratin 17 (KRT17) being expressed 
in some, but not all, basal cells [ 33, 42]. It is perhaps not 
surprising that, considering this heterogeneity among 
MTBC [43, 44], differences arise in tracheosphere-form -
ing potential when MTBC expansion medium is altered. 
It has previously been shown that primary cells grown in 
the presence of fibroblast feeder cells and ROCK inhibi -
tor retain the ability to proliferate without losing their

There are several limitations to the current study. While 
we compared three different media for the expansion of 
MTBC, we did not employ flow cytometry or single cell 
RNA sequencing to characterize the resultant subpopula-
tions of MTBC immediately following expansion in each 
medium. Additionally, cell counts and cell death were 
not analyzed in this study, so we cannot determine if the 
tracheosphere cultures with higher Mki67 expression 
also contained more cells overall compared to the other 
conditions. Although we compared three expansion 
media that have been described in the literature, we did 
not investigate how individual media components dur -
ing tracheosphere growth in 3D culture influence MTBC 
proliferation, differentiation, and expression of aberrant 
basaloid and other fibrosis relevant genes highlighted 
herein. Finally, additional studies also will be required to 
unravel how individual media components affect meta -
bolic pathways and secreted metabolites we measured 
herein and their implication for the expression of aber -
rant basaloid and other IPF relevant genes.
Conclusion
We report that the cell culture medium used during the 
expansion of MTBC dramatically affects the subsequent 
growth and differentiation potential of these cells in 3D 
culture, and should therefore be considered in studies 
involving the analysis of airway organoids. This study 
provides methodological validation for the isolation and 
expansion of MTBC for organoid culture and describes 
new endpoint analyses that can be used to characterize 
cultures in a high-throughput and cost-effective manner.
