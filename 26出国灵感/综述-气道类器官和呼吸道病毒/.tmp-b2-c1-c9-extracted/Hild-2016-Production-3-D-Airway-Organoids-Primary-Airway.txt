The ability of human airway basal cells to serve as progenitor cells in the
conducting airway makes them an attractive target in a number of respiratory
diseases associated with epithelial remodeling. This unit describes a protocol
for the culture of ‘bronchospheres’, three-dimensional (3-D) organoids that are
derived from primary human airway basal cells. Mature bronchospheres are
composed of functional multi-ciliated cells, mucin-producing goblet cells, and
airway basal cells. In contrast to existing methods used for the culture of well-
differentiated human airway epithelial cells, bronchospheres do not require
growth on a permeable support and can be cultured in 384-well assay plates.
The system provides a mechanism for investigating the regulation of basal cell
fate during airway epithelial morphogenesis, as well as a basis for studying the
function of the human airway epithelium in high-throughput assays. C⃝ 2016

INTRODUCTION
This unit describes a method (Basic Protocol 1) for the expansion of human airway basal
cells and their subsequent differentiation to form ‘bronchospheres’, organotypic cultures
of the human bronchial epithelium (Danahay et al., 2015). Bronchospheres can be used
to model growth, repair, and differentiation of the human airway epithelium without the
need for expensive and time-consuming air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures that require
the use of a permeable insert (e.g., Transwell). In addition, the system has been designed
for high-throughput applications including compound screening. Subsequent protocols
describe methods for immunostaining and imaging bronchospheres (Basic Protocol 2) as
well as RNA isolation and assessment of gene expression (Basic Protocol 3). The present
unit will not cover any details of ALI culture methodology, which have been published
elsewhere (Fulcher et al., 2005; You and Brody, 2013).
NOTE: All protocols in this unit require standard tissue culture and sterilization facilities.
Cells should be handled under sterile conditions in a Class II Biological biosafety cabinet.
NOTE: All cells are incubated at 37°C in a humidiﬁed atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air.

PROTOCOL 1
EXPANSION OF HUMAN AIRW A Y BASAL CELLS AND BRONCHOSPHERE
CULTURE
Recent methods have been described that appear to extend the capacity to expand human
airway basal cells while maintaining their potential to fully differentiate (Suprynowicz
et al., 2012; Horani et al., 2013). Ideally, airway basal cells that have undergone a minimal
number of expansion steps should be used as the progenitors for a well-differentiated
epithelium, since the number of population doublings is not unlimited. For the purposes
of this protocol, the authors have used commercially available human bronchial epithelial
cells as the source of basal cells. Details relating to the isolation and puriﬁcation of the
cells are rarely made available by vendors, and, as such, their expression of airway basal
cell markers (p63, NGFR, ITGA6) should be checked routinely. In addition, the ability of
the cells to differentiate under classical ‘air-liquid-interface’ conditions also represents
a valuable quality-control step (Fulcher et al., 2005; You and Brody, 2013).

Expansion of airway basal cells
1. Prepare growth medium by supplementing 500 ml of BEBM with the BEGM Sin-
gleQuot kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Warm 40 ml of the growth
medium to 37°C in a 150-cm2 cell culture ﬂask in a humidiﬁed 5% CO2 incubator.
Thaw a vial ( /223c500,000 cells) of airway basal cells, add to the ﬂask, and incubate
overnight.
The authors thaw one vial of Normal Human Bronchial Epithelial Cells, which has
/2265500,000 cells, for each ﬂask to be seeded. At this seeding density, the cells will reach
80% to 90% conﬂuency in /223c5 days.
2. After 24 hr, aspirate medium, add 40 ml of fresh medium, then repeat every 2 to
3 days until the cells are approximately 80% to 90% conﬂuent (Fig. IE.9.1).

3. Aspirate medium, add 10 ml of 0.05% trypsin-EDTA, and return ﬂask to the incu-
bator for 3 to 5 min until all of the cells have lifted. Add 10 ml Trypsin Neutralizing
Solution, transfer the cell suspension to a 50-ml tube, and centrifuge 5 min at 300
× g, room temperature.
4. Aspirate the neutralized trypsin off of the cell pellet and resuspend in 5 ml growth
medium or cell freezing medium (typically yields 1–2 × 106 cells/ml).
At this stage, the user has the option to cryopreserve cells or progress to seeding. The
authors typically put a vial (500,000 cells) of Normal Human Bronchial Epithelial Cells
through two rounds of expansion as described (steps 1 to 4 above), and then seed the
resulting progeny. Cells can be cryopreserved between expansions (see 5 below).
The authors would recommend assessing each batch of cells for expression of the basal
cell markers p63, ITGA6, and NGFR after each expansion, with the expectation that 100%
of cells should be p63 +ITGA6+NGFR+. See Table IE.9.1 for recommended antibodies.
In addition, the authors suggest testing cells at each expansion step to conﬁrm differ-
entiation and expression of the phenotype of interest at the air-liquid interface (Fulcher
et al., 2005; You and Brody, 2013).
5. Dilute the cell suspension from step 4 in cell freezing medium to a ﬁnal density of
500,000 cells/ml, transfer to cryovials, and freeze in 1-ml aliquots.
Preparation of bronchosphere plates
Plates can be prepared on the day that cells will be seeded, or up to 24 hr before, and
stored in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. When seeding large numbers of cells, it is suggested
that the plates be prepared the day before.
6. Defrost Matrigel on ice.
10-ml bottles of Matrige can be defrosted on ice in a cold room/refrigerator overnight.
Smaller aliquots can be defrosted on ice on the day the plates are prepared.
The authors routinely test individual Matrigel lots, with protein concentrations ranging
between 9 and 12 mg/ml and endotoxin levels less than 2 U/ml. Although we prefer to
use the same lot of Matrigel for studies, we have not observed dramatic differences in
bronchosphere formation using Matrigel from various lots with these parameters.

7. Prepare 25% (v/v) Matrigel (v/v) in differentiation medium on ice and dispense
10 μl per well of a 384-well plate at room temperature.
Maintain the Matrigel on ice prior to dilution, as it will solidify at room temperature. Do
not pre-warm the medium used to prepare the 25% solution.
F or ease, the 25% solution can be loaded into a disposable reservoir and dispensed
into the wells of the plate at room temperature using a multichannel pipettor . Once the
Matrigel is diluted to a 25% (v/v) solution, it will not solidify at room temperature, so
there is no need to pre-cool the plate or keep the plate on a cold block.
8. Centrifuge the plate 5 sec at 100 × g to ensure that all of the Matrigel is at the
bottom of the well. Place the plate in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for at least 4 hr for
the Matrigel to set.
Seeding basal cells and top-up feeds (Fig. IE.9.2)
9. Add Matrigel and cells (from step 4, above) to pre-warmed (37 °C) differentiation
medium to achieve a ﬁnal Matrigel concentration of 5% (v/v) and a cell density of
30,000 cells/ml.
10. Load the 5% Matrigel cell suspension into a disposable reservoir and dispense 20μl
per well of the Matrigel-coated 384-well plate (600 cells/well). Centrifuge the plate
for 5 sec at 50 × g (500 rpm).
A multichannel pipettor will improve efﬁciency, uniformity, and accuracy of cell seeding.
The quick spin ensures that all of the cell suspension is forced into the base of the well. It
is important that the spin be gentle to prevent cells being pushed ‘through’ the Matrigel
bed and onto the ﬂoor of the plate.
11. Place a pre-hydrated MicroClime humidiﬁed plate lid onto the plate and return it to
the 37°C incubator.
Humidiﬁed plate lids are important to reduce the risk of edge effects as a consequence of
the 2-week culture period, low-volume wells, and top-up feeds. They may not be necessary
if the user can maintain humidiﬁcation of the incubator by keeping its use to a minimum.

IMMUNOSTAINING AND IMAGING OF BRONCHOSPHERES
Immunostaining of bronchospheres is used to check for differentiation of the epithelium
as a quality-control step and as a bioassay endpoint. This protocol provides a method for
the staining and imaging of different cell types in the bronchospheres, which should be
broadly applicable to other immunoﬂuorescence assay endpoints.

RNA ISOLA TION AND ANALYSIS
Recent technological advances have made it possible to easily analyze endogenous ex-
pression of a set of genes in 384-well and even 1536-well formats. Cells can be lysed, and
without further puriﬁcation, gene expression can be measured either by high-throughput
RT-qPCR or Affymetrix’s QuantiGene 2.0 Plex Assays. In contrast to the RT-qPCR
approach, the QuantiGene assay is based purely on hybridization steps and does not
involve reverse transcription into cDNA followed by ampliﬁcation, thus eliminating the
potential biases associated with these steps. Cells or tissue samples are lysed, and the
lysates are incubated with color-coded (i.e., bead region) beads, each covalently linked
to a speciﬁc “capture probe,” a single-stranded oligonucleotide that will speciﬁcally hy-
bridize to another oligonucleotide, called the “probe extender,” which in turn hybridizes
to the target RNA (Fig. IE.9.4). RNA is detected by hybridizing a pair of “label exten-
der” oligonucleotides to the RNA of interest. The pair of label extender oligonucleotides

COMMENTARY
Background Information
The abundance and distribution of goblet
and ciliated cells in the conducting airway
must be tightly regulated to allow for nor-
mal air ﬂow. Excessive goblet cell numbers,
resulting in increased mucus production, are
a hallmark of many airway diseases such as
asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary dis-
ease (COPD), and cystic ﬁbrosis (CF). Gob-
let cells and ciliated cells are derived from a
common progenitor cell, the airway basal cell.
Identifying the mechanisms regulating airway
basal cell fate decisions to differentiate into
goblet versus ciliated cells is crucial to our un-
derstanding of airway diseases, and ultimately
to development of therapeutics that reduce
mucus production and increase mucociliary
clearance.
Studies of airway epithelial cells in vitro
have relied largely on the air-liquid interface
(ALI) method of culturing airway basal cells
from both mouse and human (Fulcher et al.,
2005; You and Brody, 2013). While ALI cul-
tures display several hallmarks of the conduct-
ing airway, particularly the repertoire of major
cell types, there are at least three factors that
limit their utility in a high-throughput format
to allow for unbiased screening. First, ALI cul-
tures are typically performed in either 12-well
or 24-well plates. Second, the medium is re-
plenished every other day. Third, published
protocols describe a culture length of 21 to
35 days to achieve a ‘fully-differentiated’ mu-
cociliary epithelium.
The authors have adapted the growth
conditions used in ALI cultures and three-
dimensional (3-D) cell culture model systems
(Debnath et al., 2003; Jaffe et al., 2008) to
allow for growth and differentiation of human
airway basal cells in a 384-well plate format to
form ‘bronchospheres’ containing basal, cili-
ated, and goblet cells. This method allows for
large-scale unbiased screening for factors that
modulate the abundance of each of these cell
types, and could be adapted for studying other
cellular processes (e.g., polarity, lumen forma-
tion) that are regulated during airway epithelial
morphogenesis.
Critical Parameters
Retinoic acid
All-trans retinoic acid is critical to the cul-
ture system, as it is required for differentiation
of airway epithelial cells into a mucociliary
epithelium (Gray et al., 1996). After generat-
ing the stock of 5 × 10−4 M all-trans retinoic
acid, the authors typically make 5- to 10- μl
aliquots and store them at −80°C, using the
appropriate number of aliquots for each ex-
periment. Repeated freeze/thaw cycles are not
recommended.
Handling of cells
The starting material and subsequent cul-
turing of cells is critical for obtaining
reproducible, well-differentiated 3-D bron-
chospheres. The authors routinely use com-
mercially available human airway basal cells
(sold as Normal Human Bronchial Epithe-
lial Cells; Lonza, cat. no. CC-2540), but pri-
mary human airway basal cells as described
in Fulcher et al. (2005) can also be used. Pas-
saging of cells on plastic, prior to plating in
3-D, should be limited to 1 to 2 passages, as
serial passaging results in the loss of mucocil-
iary differentiation potential and results in a
squamous phenotype (Araya et al., 2007). In
addition, cells grown on plastic prior to plat-
ing in 3-D should not be allowed to reach more
than 90% conﬂuence, as this also alters their
ability form reproducibly well-differentiated
bronchospheres.
